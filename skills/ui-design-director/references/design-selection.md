# Selecting the overall design concept

This phase helps the user select the overall visual structure and component character before rules or production code are produced. Palette selection is separate; read `color-selection.md` after or alongside this phase.

## 1. Create the concept brief

Derive what is safe from the repository and conversation, then confirm only consequential unknowns:

- product type, primary user, main job, and interaction archetype: marketing site, dashboard, editing tool, form workflow, or content reader;
- essential page types and the most information-dense screen;
- desired brand traits and emotions;
- trust, familiarity, distinctiveness, and conversion needs;
- mobile/desktop priority and accessibility constraints;
- existing logo, palette, typeface, component library, or technical constraints;
- explicit dislikes and examples the user considers “too much” or “too plain”;
- **what only this business has**: two or three materials, objects, places or habits of this trade (a pottery studio: clay texture, the wheel, glaze drips; a piano school: the black-and-white rhythm of keys, staff lines, the apartment-block shop sign). At least one candidate direction is built around one of them, so the site could not be mistaken for another trade;
- whether paid templates are acceptable and whether the goal is inspiration or licensed reuse.
- **photos taken in one sitting**: a restaurant's twenty dishes shot the same evening on the same black plates and table, at full phone resolution, carried both the first screen and the menu to 8.5/8 next to Naver Place and the shop the layout came from — the highest of eight roleplays. What still held it below 9 was the missing storefront and sign photo customers use to find a small shop. Ask for one session of photos of everything sold, plus the sign and the door.
- **product photos per item**: two or three per product (front, side or cut face, in a hand) taken the same way on the same surface. One matched photo per item lifted a soap shop's product pages to 7 next to running shops; the owner said 8 needs the extra angles, and a single photo plus a long table read as 「설명서」 rather than a product page. Ask for them at the brief and say what one photo per item will cap.
- **the package first**: on a product page the first photo is what the customer receives (bag, box, label), the contents second. A tea farm's product page went from 6.5 to 7 with one stand-in photo of dry leaves and stopped there: shoppers compare with the market leaders, whose first photo is the pouch, and a white studio shot sat on the cream page like a pasted square. The owner named the set that reaches 8 — package front, leaves beside it, brewed colour, all on the same table in the same light — so ask for those three by name. A stand-in product photo only measures what a photo is worth; it never ships: the same owner approved the text-only page to open (「됐다」 at 8 / 6.5) and refused the 7-point version, because on a product page — food above all — customers take any photo for the thing they will receive, and a small 「실험용 공개 사진」 caption is not read.
- **look before sending**: three avoidable misses in one bike-shop round were visible in the pictures — a commonality list that counted generously, a photo described as a workbench that was a bike by the till, and a crop that kept a handlebar and clothes instead of the bike. Open each photo and screenshot at the size the client will see it and check the words you wrote about it against it.
- **who and whose in every photo**: a fresh designer session using this skill caught a neighbour's shop sign but missed, until the owner said so, the neighbouring stall's staff presented as 「우리 가게에서 일하는 모습」, another stall's price cards on a tank, a customer's arm, and readable number plates. Before using a photo, list the people, signs, price cards and plates in it and ask the owner who and whose they are; crop rather than blur when the cropped photo still shows the thing. A food photo that shows a richer set than the cheapest menu needs a line saying which menu it is (「모둠회 중에 곁들여 나가요」).
- **describe a reference only from the capture you send**: a reference was described as listing 「lobster by weight, market price」 when that line was outside every frame sent; the owner asked where it was.
- **the five questions customers ask most on the phone** (ask the owner in those words). They become the page order, the form's questions and the facts to collect: a renovation owner had to point out, one by one, leaks being urgent, the building-office notice, how many bathrooms, the move-in date and whether the family lives there during the work — answers the designer could have had on day one.

Summarize these as **confirmed**, **inferred**, and **open**. Do not force a lengthy questionnaire when repository evidence is sufficient.

## 2. Search two kinds of source side by side

Look in two places for every project and put both in the shortlist:

- **Well-known real sites in the field** — what owners compare their result with ("compared with sites that actually run, it looks too simple") and what they can read without design vocabulary.
- **Template marketplaces** — Framer Marketplace, Webflow Templates, ThemeForest, SaaSFrame, and design-system docs for tools. Good templates are finished work: whole page sets, phone layouts, form and cart states, consistent components, and a licence to build on. They often show the screens real sites hide (sign-up, checkout, admin).

Derive the search from the brief, not from a gallery: the **field** (futsal league, piano school, pottery shop), the **job** of the main screen (find my next match, book a trial lesson, buy a mug), the **concept words** (scoreboard, warm and calm, editorial) and the **audience** (office workers on phones, parents, gift buyers). Use the same words for both kinds of source.

Real sites, best first:

1. **Leaders in the same field**, domestic and international: for a futsal league the national league and federation sites and the big league and score services (K League, Premier League, LaLiga, FotMob, ESPN) plus the leading local amateur-sport service (PLAB); for a ceramics shop the well-known studios and craft brands; for a music school the best-known academies and lesson platforms. Prefer sites with many real users or a reputation in the field over portfolio pieces.
2. **Leaders with the same main job in a neighbouring field**, when the field has few good sites: a booking leader for a class schedule, a match-centre or transit product for "what is next, where, when".
3. **Award and showcase entries in that category** (Awwwards, CSS Design Awards, Godly, Landbook filtered by Sports, Music, Shop) for one strong trait each.

Templates: search each marketplace's category and keyword for the field and the main job (sports club, league, academy, ceramics shop, booking), open the live preview of each, and judge it on the same terms as a real site — the visual layers it carries, its phone layout, and whether it covers the product's core screens. Prefer templates whose previews show several real pages over single landing pages.

Aim for a shortlist that holds both kinds — typically two to four of each — and say which is which. A real site is a motif to learn from; a template is a motif or a starting point the user could buy. Record for each what it is known for (real site) or what it covers (template), and which visual layers it carries (`reference-extraction.md` § Visual layers), because those layers are what the motif borrows. Borrow grammar and layers, never a real site's logo, illustrations, photos, text or a distinctive composition verbatim, and use a template's code or assets only within its licence; say so to the user.

When ui-ux-pro-max is installed, its style and product-pattern search can suggest directions to look for (`companion-skills.md`). A suggestion becomes a candidate only after a real site or template in that direction has been opened and inspected.

Never imply that a real site or showcase entry can be copied or purchased.

## 3. Curate candidates

Show both kinds from § 2 and mark which candidate is a running site in the field, a neighbouring-field leader, an award entry, or a template. A user without design vocabulary cannot read "just look at the mood" off a site whose content is unrelated: a piano-school owner shown a pharmacy app, a tool-rental shop and a children's English game could not picture her own page. Prefer candidates from the same or a neighbouring trade, and when the direction is still hard to read, render two or three directions with the user's own content in their language (as the layout step does) before asking them to choose; the layout step with real content was the one the user found most useful.

**Do not settle on the default reading of the brief.** Words such as “warm”, “clean” or “calm” have many readings but drift to one default: “warm” became cream paper, a brown button, a rounded heading face and a text-left/photo-right first screen for both a pottery studio and a piano school, and the two sites looked like one company's. Before presenting, write down the default you would reach for from the brief words alone, keep at most one part of it in any candidate unless the brief's own evidence calls for it (the user's wall colour, an existing sign, a font they insist on), and say which part you kept and why.

A reference from another trade can still be the right source for one trait (a train departure board for "the next match in three seconds"), but on its own it reads as irrelevant to an owner ("I'd have asked: why this?"). Show such a trait applied to the user's own content in the same step — one row of their data in that shape — rather than asking them to imagine it.

Two candidates are not optional: every site the client named (a renovation client's 「오늘의집」 was left out without a word and only explained after she asked), and at least one site that shows the thing the client called most important (before-and-after photos for a renovation shop — none of the first six showed it). If a named site cannot be captured cleanly, include it anyway with a note on what covers it.

Before sending, read the words inside every screenshot — badges (「Made in Framer」), founding years (「SINCE 1952」), certification claims (「certified organic」), sale and promo bands — against the brief's dislikes and the client's facts, and name each one first: what it is, and that none of it carries over. A tea farmer caught all four in a set where the message had described the template only as 「a free frame」. Show, next to each home page, the screen for the client's main job (booking, choosing a product) from the two or three likeliest picks; a first screen alone gave "only half the picture". When the client already has photos, put two or three of them into the likeliest candidate's structure at this step (colours still neutral) — a winter walking shot is not the summer drone shot the reference uses, and the client wants to see that before choosing.

Present 5–8 individual candidates with working detail or preview links, not gallery homepages. The set should cover 2–4 relevant concept territories while remaining plausible for the product. Avoid near-duplicates, trend-only picks, and deliberately weak decoys.

Inspect enough of each candidate to evaluate it. Prefer candidates with multiple relevant pages, responsive previews, and visible component/state coverage. A beautiful hero with no evidence for the product's core screens is a secondary visual reference, not a whole-system candidate.

For every candidate include:

- name, direct link, source, and free/paid/inspiration-only status when known;
- kind: running site in the field / leader in a neighbouring field / award entry / template, and what it is known for;
- screenshot/thumbnail when tools allow;
- concept territory and brand impression;
- why it fits the brief;
- relevant pages/components actually observed;
- one strong transferable trait;
- mismatch, risk, and expected adaptation cost;
- **whole-system**, **marketing-only**, **product-UI-only**, or **trait-only** scope.

Do not state unverified prices, licenses, responsiveness, or page coverage as fact.

## 4. Make comparison easy

Include running sites from the user's own country and language in the first set, not only famous foreign ones, show each at phone width first (that is where most of their customers look), and never send a capture with a cookie bar, popup or loading spinner over it — a bakery owner had to ask for all three. Show the candidates in the full-screen viewer described in `comparison-board.md`: capture the product's key pages at desktop and phone width, build the viewer, check it, and give the user its full address to open. Do not present a table of links or a folder of screenshots as the comparison.

Before asking, run `node scripts/skeleton.mjs formats/뼈대-390.md --width 390 name=url …` and again at 1280 on the shortlist, templates included (pages from one platform such as Naver Place count as one site), and put the shared traits in the message with their counts (「사진이 첫 화면을 채움 4/6곳」), phone and desktop separately — say them before the client asks. They tell the client where the base they pick would be changed, not what to build. Include the channels the owner named (Naver Place, Instagram, Kakao) as references; customers see those screens first.

In chat, keep to what the screens cannot show: licence and reuse status, what could not be verified, and a recommendation of one or two finalists tied to the brief. Explain why the recommendation fits the real content and user task, not merely why it looks fashionable.

Ask the user for a simple decision:

1. **one base** — the template (or site) the whole site will look and be laid out like: its type contrast, colour fields, photo placement, part shapes and page layout. When the client names several, ask which one is the base; the others become named traits. A site built only from what the shortlist shares came out the same for a café, a snack stall and a vintage shop, because every set of websites shares the same average;
2. definite rejections and why;
3. optionally one named trait from a secondary reference.

Examples of valid secondary traits: “B's type hierarchy,” “C's dashboard density,” or “D's restrained motion.” “Mix B and C” is too vague; resolve which traits govern which layer.

If no candidate fits, use the user's reactions as new constraints and return a narrower second shortlist. Do not pressure the user into choosing.

If the user explicitly delegates the choice, select the strongest candidate using the concept brief, state that the choice was delegated, and continue only through the scope they authorized.

## 5. Record the choice

Before extraction, state the proposed selection record and obtain confirmation:

- primary reference and direct URL;
- selected secondary trait(s) and their scope;
- rejected directions and the reason;
- known licensing/reuse status;
- confirmed product-specific departures from the reference;
- whether the template's original palette is rejected, retained as a finalist, or undecided.

Hold this format-selection record until the palette decision is also recorded. Together they become the first entries in `DESIGN.md`'s decision log. A requested coded preview may be created before final approval, but it is not the design system or production UI.

For a single component or minor page, two or three targeted references may replace the full shortlist. Do not use that exception for an overall product concept.
